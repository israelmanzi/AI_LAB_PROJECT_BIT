## Two Ways to Build an AI System

### Symbolic AI and Rule-Based Systems

Symbolic AI represents knowledge using symbols, facts, and logical rules. In a rule-based system, a programmer specifies instructions that determine how the system should respond to particular conditions.

The computer applies the rules to the information it receives. The system's behaviour therefore depends largely on the rules and knowledge provided by its designers.

**Advantages:**

- The rules can be inspected and understood by people.
- The system can behave predictably when its rules and inputs are clearly defined.
- Individual rules can be tested, modified, or removed.

**Limitations:**

- Designers must anticipate the situations the system may encounter.
- Rules may not cover every possible situation.
- Complex tasks can require many rules, making the system difficult to maintain.
- The system does not automatically learn new rules from examples unless a learning mechanism is added.

### Connectionist AI

Connectionist AI is an approach that represents information through networks of interconnected computational units, often called artificial neurons.

Instead of relying only on rules written by a programmer, a connectionist model can learn patterns from data. During training, a learning algorithm adjusts the model's parameters, including the weights connecting its units.

Once trained, the model can use the patterns it has learned to produce predictions or classifications for new inputs.

**Advantages:**

- Models can learn relationships between multiple features in data.
- They can generalise patterns to inputs that were not included in the training data.
- Developers do not need to specify every decision as an individual rule.

**Limitations:**

- Models often require suitable quantities of relevant training data.
- Inaccurate, incomplete, or unrepresentative data can lead to unreliable results.
- Predictions are not always correct.
- Understanding why a model produced a particular result can be difficult.

> **Important:** Symbolic AI and connectionist AI are different approaches, but they are not mutually exclusive. Some AI systems combine explicit rules with machine-learning models.

## From Biological Neurons to Artificial Neurons

A biological neuron is a specialised cell in the nervous system that receives, processes, and transmits signals. Its structure and communication processes have inspired mathematical models used in artificial neural networks.

The main structures of a typical biological neuron include:

- **Dendrites:** Receive signals from other cells.
- **Cell body (soma):** Maintains the cell and integrates incoming signals.
- **Axon:** Carries electrical signals away from the cell body.
- **Synapses:** Junctions through which neurons communicate with other cells.

Biological neurons are complex living cells. Artificial neurons are simplified mathematical models and should not be understood as exact copies of biological neurons.

An artificial neuron receives numerical inputs, assigns a weight to each input, combines the weighted inputs, and applies an activation function to calculate an output.

A simplified mathematical representation is:

`output = activation(weighted sum of inputs + bias)`

A **weight** determines how strongly an input influences the calculation. A **bias** is an additional adjustable parameter that shifts the calculation. An **activation function** transforms the combined value into the neuron's output.

During training, a learning algorithm can adjust the weights and biases to improve the model's performance on a particular task.

Artificial neurons can be connected in layers to form a neural network. These networks use adjustable parameters to learn patterns from data.

### Biological and Artificial Neurons Compared

| Biological neuron | Artificial neuron |
|---|---|
| Receives signals through dendrites and synapses | Receives numerical inputs |
| Synaptic connections influence communication between neurons | Weights determine the influence of inputs |
| Processes incoming signals through biological mechanisms | Calculates a weighted combination of inputs |
| Transmits signals through its axon | Produces a numerical output for further computation |

This comparison helps explain the inspiration behind artificial neural networks. However, biological neurons and artificial neurons differ substantially in their structure, mechanisms, and complexity.

## How Does a Model Learn?

Machine learning is a field of artificial intelligence in which algorithms use data to identify patterns and build models that can make predictions, classifications, or other decisions.

Rather than requiring a programmer to define every possible outcome in advance, machine learning uses information in the available data to adjust a model's parameters. The resulting model can then be evaluated on data that was not used during training.

### Training Data and Labels

**Training data** is the information used by a learning algorithm to train a model. It may contain numerical measurements, text, images, or other types of information, depending on the task.

A **label** is the known category or target answer associated with an example in a labelled dataset.

The information provided to a model is often called its **input**, while the answer it is trained to predict is called its **target**. In supervised learning, training examples provide both inputs and their corresponding targets.

### Supervised Learning

Supervised learning trains a model using labelled examples. During training, the model produces predictions, and a learning algorithm uses the differences between those predictions and the target answers to update the model's parameters.

The goal is to learn patterns that help the model make useful predictions for new, previously unseen inputs.

Supervised learning is commonly used for:

- **Classification:** Assigning an input to a category.
- **Regression:** Predicting a numerical value.

The quality of the training data and the suitability of the model affect how well it performs. A model that performs well on its training examples does not necessarily perform well on new data.

### Unsupervised Learning

Unsupervised learning works with data that does not contain supplied target labels. Instead, an algorithm attempts to identify patterns, structures, or similarities within the data.

One common technique is **clustering**, which groups data points according to similarities identified by the algorithm.

Unlike supervised learning, unsupervised learning does not use known target answers to guide the model towards a specific prediction during training. The patterns discovered may require further analysis to determine whether they are meaningful or useful.

## A Short History of Neural Networks

Research into artificial neural networks has developed through several important milestones.

| Year | Milestone | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1943 | McCulloch–Pitts neuron | Introduced a mathematical model of a simplified artificial neuron. |
| 1958 | Rosenblatt's perceptron | Presented a trainable neural model for certain classification tasks. |
| Late 1960s onwards | Reduced interest in some neural-network approaches | Limitations of early models contributed to a decline in research interest. |
| 1986 | Backpropagation popularised | Helped establish a practical method for training multi-layer neural networks. |
| 2006 onwards | Renewed interest in deep learning | Advances in training methods and computing helped encourage research into deeper networks. |
| 2012 | AlexNet's ImageNet result | Demonstrated the strong potential of deep neural networks for large-scale image recognition. |

The history of artificial intelligence includes periods often described as **AI winters**, when enthusiasm, investment, and research activity declined. The history of neural networks is connected to this broader story, but a decline in neural-network research and an AI winter are not exactly the same thing.

The development of modern deep learning was not caused by a single breakthrough. It benefited from improvements in algorithms, computing power, available data, and neural-network architectures.
